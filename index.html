About Me

Background

I'm a first-year at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, class of 2030, majoring in Biology on the pre-med track and considering a Business in Health minor. I was born in the Philippines and lived there until I was six, when I moved to Raleigh to join my parents. Those two places left me with a question I keep returning to: who gets care, and how is it organized to reach them?

For three years, mostly during summers and often past 30 hours a week, I worked at Halo, a small regenerative health and wellness clinic built largely around peptide therapies. I started as an intern and became a medical assistant. Some days meant running labs, taking vitals, and preparing injection mixtures. Others were administrative: prior authorizations, the physicians' inboxes, helping set up the office itself. I also trained a new employee to take over my work. Near the end, I used basic coding to build an inventory management system. Until then, tracking which patients had ordered, and who needed a message reminding them to buy more, was done by hand, which made it easy to miss someone. I built the system to replace that process. Running a clinic, I learned, is as much logistics as medicine.

Outside of school, I've put the most into fencing. I fenced épée for five years and earned my B rating, which took me to the Junior Olympics and the Division II Summer Nationals. At one tournament, I hurt my hand and thumb early on and chose to keep going. I fenced my way past two rounds of direct elimination before losing. The pain forced me to fence differently, so I had to read each situation quickly and find new ways to get the touches I wanted. I expect a busy clinic to ask for the same adaptability.

Future Goals

Next, I'd like to join a stem cell research lab and learn the mechanisms behind newer work on regeneration and injury recovery, the science underneath what I watched patients hope for at Halo. I also plan to study abroad, and Scotland interests me most. Its public health system works on different incentives than the private clinic I know, and I want to see what that changes about who gets seen, how quickly, and at what cost.

Eventually, I want to be a physician and open a clinic in the Philippines, where I still have family. Many of them wait in long lines just to be seen, and when their turn comes, the visit is short, so there is little time for a doctor to listen or explain. The Business in Health minor, if I take it, is meant for this goal: running a clinic well is what keeps it open long enough to matter.
